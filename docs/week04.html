Week 4

Measurement and Error

Introduction

Imagine four university friends are taking the unit: Statistical Thinking. Throughout the semester, they have usually done quite well. Their final exam is scheduled for Monday morning. However, the day before the exam, they partied too hard, sleept in, and miss the exam. They decide to write to their professor and ask for a chance to sit a make-up exam. Their email says:

This morning, on our way to campus, one of the car tyres was punctured. We had to pull over on the side of the road. Because it was raining, it was difficult to change the tyre. We were eventually able to change it, but this is why we were late to campus and missed the exam.

Assume you are the professor and your task is to rate this excuse. Use a scale from 1 (not believable at all) to 10 (completely believable). You only need to submit one response.

The professor accepted the students’ excuse and allowed all four of them to sit a make-up exam.

But there was a catch:

  • The professor placed each student in a different room.
  • Each student received a single exam paper.
  • On the front page were a few standard statistics questions worth 5 marks in total.
  • After completing those questions, the students reached a note telling them to turn over the page.
  • On the second page, there was only one question, and it was worth 95 marks:
Which tyre was it?


What did we just measure?

At first, this activity looks like a simple rating task. Each person read the same excuse and gave it a score from 1 to 10. But from a statistical point of view, something more interesting happened.

Everyone saw the same information, but not everyone gave the same rating.

  • Some people may have found the excuse believable.
  • Others may have been more suspicious.
  • Some may have focused on the punctured tyre.
  • Others may have focused on the fact that all four students missed the same exam.

This means the rating is not simply a fact. It is a measurement produced by a judgement process.

We can think of each rating as:

\[ \text{Observed rating} = \text{Perceived believability} + \text{Rating error} \]

The phrase rating error does not mean you made a mistake. It means that the rating depends on the person making the judgement, the assumptions they bring, and the criteria they use.

Some of this variation may be random. For example, one student may happen to focus on the rain, while another focuses on the timing of the excuse. Some of the variation may be systematic. For example, some students may be generally more trusting, while others may be consistently more sceptical.

This is why ratings are useful for thinking about measurement. They look numerical, but they are still produced by a process.

NoteQuestion 6: Mean error and absolute error

The mean error tells us whether ratings tend to be above or below the reference rating.

The mean absolute error tells us how far ratings are from the reference rating on average, ignoring whether they are above or below.

Complete the code below.

excuse_ratings |>

    mean_absolute_error = ___

The function abs() returns the absolute value. This removes the negative signs, so errors above and below the reference rating do not cancel out.

NoteQuestion 10: Interpret the measurement process

Answer the following questions in words.

  1. Which class gave the excuse a higher average rating?
  2. Which class had more variation in ratings?
  3. What might cause one class to be more sceptical than another?
  4. What would count as random error in this rating process?
  5. What would count as systematic error?
  6. Would a rubric make the ratings more consistent?
  7. Could a rubric also introduce bias?
ImportantClick for discussion

Possible answers:

  1. The original class should have the higher average rating.
  2. The class with the larger standard deviation has more variation.
  3. One class may be more sceptical because of prior experience, classroom culture, how the story was framed, or how seriously they view missing an exam.
  4. Random error could include students noticing different details, interpreting the scale differently, or being in different moods.
  5. Systematic error could include some students being consistently harsher, consistently more trusting, or consistently influenced by details such as rain, carpooling, or the fact that all four students missed the exam.
  6. A rubric might reduce variation by making the criteria clearer.
  7. A rubric could introduce bias if it rewards some features of the excuse and ignores others.

NoteQuestion 17: Interpret the normal models

Answer the following questions in words.

  1. In the normal model \(X \sim N(5.5, 1.8)\), what does 5.5 represent?
  2. What does 1.8 represent?
  3. What does dnorm() calculate?
  4. What does pnorm() calculate?
  5. What does qnorm() calculate?
  6. What does rnorm() do?
  7. Why might a normal model be imperfect for ratings from 1 to 10?
  8. What would a larger standard deviation mean in this context?
ImportantClick for discussion

Possible answers:

  1. The value 5.5 represents the centre, or mean, of the rating process.
  2. The value 1.8 represents the standard deviation, or spread, of the ratings.
  3. dnorm() calculates the height of the normal curve at a given value.
  4. pnorm() calculates the probability of being below a given value.
  5. qnorm() calculates the value corresponding to a given cumulative probability.
  6. rnorm() generates random values from a normal distribution.
  7. A normal model is imperfect because ratings are bounded between 1 and 10, often whole numbers, and may be influenced by judgement, context, and class discussion.
  8. A larger standard deviation would mean more disagreement between students. Ratings would be more spread out.

Homework

In the tutorial, we treated believability ratings as measurements produced by a judgement process. For homework, you will extend this idea by building a small simulation study. Your goal is to investigate how different kinds of rating behaviour affect the results we see.

The scenario

A class is asked to rate the students’ excuse from 1 to 10.

Suppose there is an underlying average believability level of:

\[ \mu = 5.5 \]

This does not mean the excuse has a true objective rating of 5.5. It is just a modelling assumption: in a large group, ratings tend to centre around 5.5.

However, different classes may rate the same excuse differently depending on the measurement process.

You will compare three possible rating processes.

Rating process Description
Process A A balanced class: ratings are centred around 5.5 with moderate variation.
Process B A sceptical class: ratings are shifted downward.
Process C A divided class: half the students are sceptical and half are trusting.
