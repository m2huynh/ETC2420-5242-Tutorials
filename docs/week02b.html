Week 2

Statistical Thinking

Introduction

This week we begin with a short witness activity.

You will watch a brief animated scene only once. Your task is to pay attention to what happens and then answer a series of recall questions. Some questions will be about the main event. Others will be about background details, such as objects in the room, people nearby, or things that were easy to overlook.

After the scene, you will answer each recall question and give a confidence rating. This gives us two kinds of data:

  • whether your answer was correct
  • how confident you were in that answer

This lets us ask better statistical questions. Instead of only asking:

What happened?

we can ask:

How reliable was the witness evidence?

Did confidence match accuracy?

Which details were easiest or hardest to remember?

How much variation was there across witnesses?

Part 2: Accuracy and confidence

A natural first question is:

How many questions did I get right?

But a better statistical question is:

How accurate was my recall, and how confident was I in that recall?

Part 3: Did confidence match accuracy?

One useful way to think about witness evidence is to compare confidence with accuracy.

If someone gives a high-confidence answer, we might be tempted to treat that answer as strong evidence. But in real life, confidence and accuracy do not always match perfectly.

Homework

Scenario: Dr.Arbuthnot’s baptism records

John Arbuthnot was a Scottish physician. In 1710 he published a paper1 that examined birth records in London for each of the 82 years from 1629 to 1710. Actually the data he used were counts of baptisms, by year and gender, but these are used as a proxy for the number of births.

Data

The data from Arbuthnot’s study is contained in the file arbuthnot.csv. It consists of three columns, named year, boys and girls. The boys and girls columns show the number of each that were born in London for each year as specified in the year column.


Download and transfer the file arbuthnot.csv to your Project folder > week 2 > data

Tasks

  1. What line of R code would you use to extract the vector containing just the counts of girls baptised? Would it be a good idea to print it in an assignment?

  2. Using ggplot(), create a plot to visualise the number of girls baptised per year. Is there an apparent trend in the number of girls baptized over the years? How would you describe it? Are there any interesting features?

  3. Create three new columns in your data:

    • total: the sum of boy baptisims and girl baptisms
    • ratio: the number of girl baptisms divided by the number of boy baptisms
    • prop_girls: the proportion of girl baptisms

  4. Draw a line plot of the total number of baptisms per year. How often were there more boys born than girls?

  5. Draw a plot of the proportion of boys born over time. What do you see? Discuss this plot. Can it be improved?

  6. Use the internet or help in R to create a TERRIBLE graph, which has none of the good attributes from the previous question. Comment on what makes it terrible.
